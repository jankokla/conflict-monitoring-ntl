    Introduction

    Satellite observations of the Earth’s nighttime illumination, referred to as nighttime light (NTL), provide a unique vantage point for analyzing global patterns and changes in human activity, infrastructure, and environmental impact. Numerous studies have shown that NTL can be applied to monitor slow changing human phenomena, such as mapping urban areas 1, estimating socioeconomic parameters 2 and quantifying light pollution 3. These insights rely on a growing suite of satellite sensors specifically designed to capture variations in nighttime illumination.

    Among them, NASA’s Black Marble has become the \textit{de facto} standard product for global nighttime light analysis, providing openly accessible daily radiance data from the VIIRS Day–Night Band at 500 m resolution 4. In contrast, the Sustainable Development Goals Science Satellite 1 (SDGSAT‑1), developed by the International Research Center of Big Data for Sustainable Development Goals (CBAS), captures panchromatic imagery with its Glimmer Imager at a much finer 10 m resolution 5, revealing detailed urban and infrastructural features. Other missions primarily designed for daytime observations, such as EnMAP 67 and Landsat‑8 8, have also been applied to the monitoring of nighttime illumination.

    These advances in spatial and temporal resolution have expanded nighttime light research beyond long-term urbanization and economic studies to the detection of rapid, short-term events. In particular, advances in temporal resolution now allow sensors to support near real‑time monitoring of rapidly evolving events, including conflicts. Building on this, a growing body of work shows that conflicts typically lead to declines in nighttime lights, reflecting destroyed infrastructure 9, disrupted energy supplies 10, curfews that restrict human activity 11, and the deliberate extinguishing of lights to avoid detection 12. At the same time, specific processes such as rescue operations or fires following attacks can locally increase NTL in affected areas 13.

    Despite the new potential, NTL-based conflict monitoring faces fundamental limitations related to its dependence on existing lighting infrastructure. Armed violence disproportionately affects low-income countries and economically marginalized regions 14, and an estimated 1.18 billion people in the developing world lack electricity and therefore detectable NTL 15. While the feasibility of NTL-based monitoring has been demonstrated in in urban regions 9, subsequent work 16 indicates that nearly one third of people affected by violence live in areas without detectable NTL, making it difficult to monitor potential declines in nighttime illumination.

    The aim of this project is to evaluate the suitability of several nighttime light (NTL) satellite products for conflict monitoring applications, with a particular focus on the conditions under which they can reliably capture conflict-related disruptions. Specifically, we assess four products – Black Marble, SDGSAT‑1, EnMAP, and Landsat‑8 – using a comprehensive set of criteria that reflect the requirements of armed-conflict monitoring. A unified data-processing pipeline is developed to harmonize these heterogeneous sensors, enabling consistent comparisons and facilitating future multi-sensor NTL analyses. Beyond sensor performance, the study also highlights regional disparities in how NTL relates to different types of built-up environments and development levels, thereby identifying where baseline radiance patterns can be established and where sudden declines are most likely to indicate conflict-driven disruption.
